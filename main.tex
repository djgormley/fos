\section*{}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{yclostationarity} is usually introduced through second- and higher-order statistics, so first order is easy to overlook.  At first order, the cyclic moment is the cyclic mean: counter-rotate the record and average.  The same calculation gives a discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) sample and a complex-tone correlation, each divided by $N$; on the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) grid, it is $X[k]/N$.  Under the white-Gaussian tone model stated below, its noise-normalized energy is also a generalized likelihood-ratio test (GLRT) statistic for an unknown complex amplitude.

This is not a new detector.  The useful point is that a first-order cyclic feature can be implemented and analyzed with familiar tone-detection tools.  Three conditions should be kept separate: the FFT grid affects implementation, the signal and noise model determine optimality, and an ensemble cyclic-mean interpretation requires a stable phase reference and suitable averaging assumptions.  This article derives the connection and turns these distinctions into a practical guide.

\section*{The Core Operation: Counter-Rotate, Then Average}

Consider the complex record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, and let $\alpha$ be the candidate frequency in cycles per sample, understood modulo one.  An ordinary mean extracts zero frequency, so a nonzero-frequency component tends to cancel as its phase rotates.  Counter-rotation moves a candidate component at $\alpha$ to zero frequency:
\begin{equation*}
z_{\alpha}[n]\equiv x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\end{equation*}
The cyclic-mean estimate is then the ordinary mean of the corrected samples:
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z_{\alpha}[n]
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\label{eq:cyclicmean}
\end{equation}
Here, $M_x^{\alpha}$ is the limiting time-average coefficient, while $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ is its finite-record estimate.  Under a suitable consistency condition, the time-average coefficient also equals the corresponding ensemble coefficient.  The decision statistic $T_{\alpha}$ is defined below.  For $x[n]=ae^{j2\pi\alpha n}$, every corrected sample equals $a$, so $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=a$.  At a mismatched frequency, the corrected samples still rotate and partially cancel.  This estimator is also used directly in cyclostationary signal detection and classification~\cite[eq. (9)]{dobre2009}.

\textbf{Fourier and correlator views.}
Writing the phase-correction weights as vectors makes the connection explicit.  Collect the record and candidate phase progression in
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vect{x}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
x[0] & x[1] & \cdots & x[N-1]
\end{bmatrix}^{T},\\
\vect{s}_{\alpha}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & e^{j2\pi\alpha} & \cdots & e^{j2\pi\alpha(N-1)}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The entries of $\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}$ are the phase-correction factors in~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean}.  Let $X_N(\alpha)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}$ denote the length-$N$ DTFT evaluation.  The three views are therefore
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\frac{X_N(\alpha)}{N}
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}{N}.
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
On the DFT grid, $\alpha\equiv k/N\pmod 1$, so $X_N(\alpha)=X[k]$ and $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=X[k]/N$, where
\begin{equation}
X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}.
\end{equation}
Off grid, the sum in~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean} is still exact; it is a DTFT sample rather than an $N$-point DFT bin.  Zero padding supplies exact DTFT samples on a denser grid, while interpolation or refinement estimates an arbitrary off-grid location.  This is the usual FFT-grid issue, not a limitation of the first-order identity.  Gardner's time-average Fourier coefficients give the corresponding infinite-record view~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.

\textbf{The tip.} When the feature of interest is first order, no specialized cyclostationary processing block is needed: compute the familiar tone correlation in~\eqref{eq:chain}.  Under the white-Gaussian model below, its noise-normalized squared magnitude is the GLRT statistic for a tone, so standard thresholds and detection curves apply.

\section*{Why First Order Is the Cyclic Mean}

Start with the continuous-time $p$th-order cyclic temporal cross-moment~\cite[eq. (13.1)]{gardner2006}:
\begin{equation}
R_{x,p}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=
\left\langle
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}
x_{\ell}^{(*)_{\ell}}(t+\tau_{\ell})
e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}
\right\rangle_t ,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
Here, $p$ is the moment order, $(*)_{\ell}$ denotes optional conjugation, and $\langle\cdot\rangle_t$ denotes a time average.  The full form uses $p$ delays; its reduced form uses $p-1$ relative lags.  At $p=1$, the product has one factor and no relative lag, so the first-order cyclic moment is simply the cyclic mean.  With no relative lag, there is no lag variable to Fourier transform.

At first order, conjugation adds no new information when both signs of cycle frequency are included:
\begin{equation*}
\left\langle x^*(t+\tau)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=
\left[
\left\langle x(t+\tau)e^{+j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
\right]^* .
\end{equation*}
Thus, the conjugated coefficient at $\alpha$ is the complex conjugate of the unconjugated coefficient at $-\alpha$.  For real-valued $x$, the two forms are identical at the same cycle frequency.

First order also has no independent lag.  The delay $\tau$ shifts the only signal factor rather than measuring separation between factors.  Shift invariance of the infinite-time average gives
\begin{equation*}
R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(\tau)
=\left\langle x(t+\tau)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)e^{j2\pi\alpha\tau}.
\end{equation*}
Thus, $\tau$ changes only a known phase.  The reduced-dimension convention sets $\tau=0$ without losing information~\cite[eq. (13.7)]{gardner2006}, giving
\begin{equation}
M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\left\langle x(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\label{eq:cyclicmeanct}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean} is the rectangular, $N$-sample estimate of this coefficient.  It estimates the ensemble coefficient consistently only when the records share a phase reference and the appropriate first-order cycloergodicity or consistency condition holds.

\section*{When the Common Output Is a GLRT}

The identities above are algebraic and require no noise model.  The GLRT interpretation requires the following signal and noise model~\cite[Sec. 13.4.1]{kay1998}:
\begin{align}
\mathcal H_0 &: \vect{x}=\vect{w},\nonumber\\
\mathcal H_1 &: \vect{x}=a\vect{s}_{\alpha}+\vect{w},
\qquad
\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\sigma^2\vect{I}),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
Here, $\alpha$ and $\sigma^2$ are known, while the deterministic complex amplitude $a$ is unknown.  The noise is proper complex white Gaussian, with $\E\{\vect{w}\vect{w}^{H}\}=\sigma^2\vect{I}$ and $\E\{\vect{w}\vect{w}^{T}\}=\vect{0}$.  Apart from constants, the log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\ell(a)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
\left\|\vect{x}-a\vect{s}_{\alpha}\right\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Because $\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}=N$, maximizing the likelihood gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat a
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\widehat M_x^{\alpha},
\label{eq:ahat}
\end{equation}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:ahat} gives a GLRT that is monotone in
\begin{equation}
T_{\alpha}
=\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}
{\sigma^2\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\frac{N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}.
\label{eq:glrt}
\end{equation}

This result is exact for any finite $N$.  Under~\eqref{eq:model}, the same number is noise-normalized cyclic-mean energy, a normalized periodogram ordinate, and the usual noncoherent GLRT statistic for a tone.  This tone-detection connection is also developed from matched-filter and Fourier viewpoints in~\cite[p. 600]{spooner2009}.

The covariance-normalized cyclic-feature test gives the same decision rule~\cite[eqs. (B.1)--(B.3)]{dobre2009}.  Define
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\vect{m}}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\Re\{\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\} &
\Im\{\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{equation}
Under $\mathcal H_0$,
$\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{\vect{m}})=\sigma^2\vect{I}_2/(2N)$, so
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\vect{m}}^{T}
\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{\vect{m}})^{-1}
\widehat{\vect{m}}
=\frac{2N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}
=2T_{\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the covariance statistic is $2T_{\alpha}$; the factor of two changes only the threshold scale, not the rejection region.  Kay's complex-envelope sinusoid GLRT uses the same convention~\cite[eqs. (13.49)--(13.53)]{kay1998}.

Under $\mathcal H_0$, $T_{\alpha}$ has a unit-mean exponential distribution, so
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{FA}}=e^{-\gamma},
\qquad
\gamma=-\log P_{\mathrm{FA}}.
\label{eq:pfa}
\end{equation}
\begin{samepage}
The direct implementation has three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Counter-rotate and sum: $u_{\alpha}=\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}$.
\item Normalize: $T_{\alpha}=|u_{\alpha}|^2/(N\sigma^2)$.
\item Threshold: decide $\mathcal H_1$ when $T_{\alpha}>\gamma$.
\end{enumerate}
This threshold is exact for one preassigned $\alpha$ and known $\sigma^2$.  Estimating $\sigma^2$ changes the null distribution and threshold.
\end{samepage}
Under $\mathcal H_1$,
\begin{equation}
2T_{\alpha}\sim
\chi'^2_2\!\left(\frac{2N|a|^2}{\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:noncentral}
\end{equation}
which gives the standard noncoherent matched-filter detection probability.  Classical tone-detection performance expressions therefore apply directly to $T_{\alpha}$.

If the complex amplitude $a$ is fully known, the Neyman--Pearson test instead uses the coherent statistic $\Re\{a^*\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}\}$.  The estimate $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ is still a scaled matched-filter output, but the final scalar test depends on which signal parameters are unknown.

\section*{Numerical Verification and Grid Mismatch}

To verify the identity, we simulate a length-$64$ complex tone in white noise and compute $T_{\alpha}$ in two ways: from the cyclic mean and from the matched filter.  The largest absolute difference is below $5\times10^{-12}$.  We then perform $80{,}000$ Monte Carlo trials at each SNR value, with $P_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.01$, random seed 20260902, and per-sample SNR $\rho=|a|^2/\sigma^2$.  Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(a) follows the predicted noncentral chi-square law.  The script and version-pinned dependencies are available at \url{https://github.com/djgormley/fos}.

Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(b) shows the familiar FFT-grid mismatch.  For the rectangular $N=64$ record, a tone halfway between two bins retains the exact power fraction $[N\sin(\pi/(2N))]^{-2}$, a $3.92$-dB loss when evaluated at one bin.  Evaluating the cyclic mean at the tone's true frequency has no mismatch loss.  Zero padding, interpolation, or frequency refinement provides the same remedies used in higher-order cyclic-spectrum processing.

\section*{Where the Equivalence Needs Care}

The algebraic identity requires no stochastic model.  Unknown frequency, colored noise, and real-valued data change the detector implementation.  Random phase instead affects whether the output represents a nonzero ensemble cyclic mean.

\textbf{Unknown frequency.}
If the frequency is unknown, we maximize~\eqref{eq:glrt} over a candidate set:
\begin{equation}
T=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal A}
\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}{N\sigma^2}.
\end{equation}
This is a bank of tone matched filters, or a peak-periodogram detector.  When $\mathcal A$ is the DFT grid, an FFT performs the search.  Because several frequencies are tested, the single-frequency threshold in~\eqref{eq:pfa} no longer applies.

\textbf{Colored noise.}
For colored noise with known positive-definite covariance $\vect{C}$ and $\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\vect{C})$, the GLRT becomes
\begin{equation}
T_C=
\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{x}|^2}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}.
\label{eq:colored}
\end{equation}
This is the usual matched filter after whitening both the record and the template.  The raw cyclic-mean or DFT output is generally not optimal.  It gives the same rejection regions, up to a positive scale, only when $\vect{s}_{\alpha}$ is an eigenvector of $\vect{C}$; examples are white noise and DFT-grid tones under a circulant covariance model.

\textbf{Real tones.}
For a real sinusoid of known frequency but unknown amplitude and phase in real white Gaussian noise, the GLRT uses both the sine and cosine components.  At a non-DC, non-Nyquist DFT bin, these components are orthogonal and the statistic is proportional to $|X[k]|^2$.  Off grid, they are generally not orthogonal, so the full two-component Gram-matrix normalization is required.

\textbf{Random ensemble phase.}
A finite record can contain a detectable tone even when its ensemble cyclic mean is zero.  Let
\begin{equation}
X[n]=Ae^{j(2\pi\alpha_0 n+\Phi)}+W[n],
\qquad \Phi\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi),
\label{eq:randomphase}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is deterministic, $W[n]$ is zero mean, and $\Phi$ is drawn independently for each realization but held fixed within it.  Because $\E\{e^{j\Phi}\}=0$, we have $\E\{X[n]\}=0$.  Within one realization, however, the phase is fixed and, ignoring noise,
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}=Ae^{j\Phi},
\end{equation}
and $|\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}|^2$ remains detectable.  Thus, complex averaging across independently phased records tends to zero, whereas averaging squared magnitudes preserves the tone energy.

Keep the three claims separate: the finite-record identity is always algebraically exact; the GLRT interpretation requires the stated signal and noise model; and an ensemble cyclic-mean interpretation additionally requires phase coherence or an appropriate cycloergodicity condition.

\section*{Why the Connection Is Useful}

Second- and higher-order cyclic statistics can reveal periodic dependence even when the ordinary mean is zero~\cite[eqs. (8.1)--(8.3)]{gardner2006}.  First order captures periodicity in the mean; its coefficients describe spectral-line components~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.  Simpler does not mean empty: first-order features have been used, for example, to detect and classify AM and FSK signals~\cite{dobre2009}.

The identity translates among cyclic-mean estimation, Fourier analysis, and tone matched filtering.  For a known or searched first-order component---such as a carrier, pilot, residual spectral line, or modulation-dependent mean---an ordinary FFT or tone correlator can be used.  Existing mismatch analysis, thresholds, and performance expressions carry over once the model and normalizations agree.  The GLRT also provides an exact benchmark for checking an implementation.

The connection also helps diagnose a missed feature: possible causes include grid mismatch, colored noise, and loss of phase coherence.  If different records have independent random phases, complex averaging cancels the cyclic mean; noncoherent magnitude-square combining or a higher-order cyclic statistic may then be more appropriate.

\section*{Conclusion}

At first order, the reduced-dimension cyclic moment is the cyclic mean.  For a rectangular $N$-sample record, its finite-record estimate is
\begin{equation*}
\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
\equiv\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\frac{X_N(\alpha)}{N}
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}{N}.
\end{equation*}
In words, estimating the first-order cyclic moment, evaluating the finite-record DTFT, and correlating with a complex tone produce the same complex output after normalization.

\begin{samepage}
Under the known-frequency, unknown-complex-amplitude white-Gaussian model in~\eqref{eq:model}, the normalized energy of this common output is the tone matched-filter GLRT statistic:
\begin{equation*}
\boxed{
T_{\alpha}
=\frac{N|\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)|^2}{\sigma^2}.
}
\end{equation*}
\end{samepage}
Thus, the tone matched-filter GLRT statistic is the first-order cyclic-moment energy measured in noise units.  This equality holds at every evaluated $\alpha$; only the $N$-point DFT-bin shortcut requires $\alpha\equiv k/N\pmod 1$.  Equivalently, $|\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)|=(\sigma/\sqrt{N})\sqrt{T_{\alpha}}$, so $T_{\alpha}$ determines the estimate's magnitude but not its phase.  With known colored-noise covariance, the covariance-weighted statistic $T_C$ in~\eqref{eq:colored} replaces this white-noise form.

The practical lesson is simple: under the stated model, first-order cyclic detection is ordinary tone detection written in cyclic-statistics notation.
