\section*{}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{yclostationarity} is usually introduced through second- and higher-order statistics, so first order is easy to overlook.  At first order, a reduced-dimension cyclic moment is a cyclic-mean coefficient.  For the complex-correlation configuration used here, the complex conjugate of its finite-record estimate at cycle frequency $\alpha$ is exactly the $1/N$-normalized output of a matched filter for a complex tone at frequency $-\alpha$.

The same output is a finite-record Fourier evaluation and, on the transform grid, an inverse discrete Fourier transform (IDFT) output.  These are not separate operations: they are the same projection written with different sign, conjugation, and normalization conventions.  The identity is algebraic and requires no noise or statistical model.  This article derives it from the general cyclic-moment definition and separates the exact identity from the grid used to implement it.

\section*{Why First Order Becomes a Cyclic Mean}

A $p$th-order cyclic moment averages a product of $p$ possibly conjugated and shifted signal factors after counter-rotation.  The single-signal continuous-time definition is~\cite[eq. (13.1)]{gardner2006}
\begin{equation}
R_{x,p}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=
\left\langle
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}
x^{(*)_{\ell}}(t+\tau_{\ell})
e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}
\right\rangle_t ,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
where $(*)_{\ell}$ denotes optional conjugation and $\langle\cdot\rangle_t$ denotes a time average.  Setting $p=1$ leaves one factor and no relative lags.  We use the complex-correlation configuration in which that factor is $x^*$ and denote its coefficient by $M_x^\alpha$.  The remaining reference delay changes only a known phase:
\begin{equation}
R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(\tau)
=\left\langle x^*(t+\tau)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=e^{j2\pi\alpha\tau}R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0).
\label{eq:firstorderlag}
\end{equation}
The reduced-dimension convention therefore sets the reference delay to zero without losing information~\cite[eq. (13.7)]{gardner2006}:
\begin{equation}
M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\left\langle x^*(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\label{eq:firstorderzero}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:firstorderzero} defines the conjugated cyclic-mean convention used below.  The common unstarred convention is~\cite[eq. (2)]{gardner1986}
\begin{equation*}
\left\langle x(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=\left[M_x^{-\alpha}\right]^*;
\end{equation*}
thus, the two first-order configurations determine one another by frequency reflection and conjugation.

\section*{From Cyclic Mean to Tone Matched-Filter Output}

Consider the complex record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, with $\alpha$ measured in cycles per sample and understood modulo one.  For an unweighted (rectangular-windowed) record, the selected first-order estimate is
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^*[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\label{eq:cyclicmean}
\end{equation}
The operation is easy to picture: conjugate the record, counter-rotate by $\alpha$, and average.  If $x[n]=ae^{-j2\pi\alpha n}$, every corrected sample equals $a^*$ and $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=a^*$.  At a mismatched frequency, the corrected samples continue to rotate and partially cancel.

Define the ordinary finite-record Fourier evaluation and the length-$N$ tone vector by
\begin{align}
X_N(f)&\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi fn},
\label{eq:finitefourier}\\
\vect{s}_{f}&\equiv
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & e^{j2\pi f} & \cdots & e^{j2\pi f(N-1)}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\label{eq:tonevector}
\end{align}
Conjugating~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean} reverses the exponential sign.  Because $\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{-\alpha}=N$, the complete translation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left[\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\right]^*
&=\left[\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)\right]^*\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{+j2\pi\alpha n}\\
&=\frac{X_N(-\alpha)}{N}
=\frac{\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}
       {\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{-\alpha}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
The last expression is the projection coefficient onto the tone at $-\alpha$.  Define the raw matched-filter output as
\begin{equation*}
y_{\mathrm{MF}}(-\alpha)\equiv\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{x},
\end{equation*}
so the central identity is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
y_{\mathrm{MF}}(-\alpha)
=N\left[\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\right]^*
=N\left[\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)\right]^* .
}
\label{eq:matchedoutput}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{deepblue}{warmgray}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\textbf{The tip.} To obtain the selected first-order cyclic moment at cycle frequency $\alpha$, correlate the original record with a tone at frequency $-\alpha$, divide by $N$, and conjugate.  Conversely, multiply the conjugated moment by $N$ to recover the raw tone matched-filter output.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

\section*{The Transform Grid Is Only an Implementation Choice}

Let $X[k]\equiv X_N(k/N)$ denote the ordinary $N$-point discrete Fourier transform (DFT).  When $\alpha\equiv k/N\pmod 1$, the normalized matched-filter output is an IDFT sample, or equivalently a DFT sample with its bin label reversed:
\begin{equation}
\left[\widehat M_x^{k/N}\right]^*
=\operatorname{IDFT}_N\{\vect{x}\}[k]
=\frac{X[(-k)\bmod N]}{N}.
\label{eq:idft}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:chain} is exact at every evaluated $\alpha$; a transform grid is required only for the IDFT/DFT shortcut.  Off grid, evaluate $X_N(-\alpha)$ or the tone correlation directly.  Zero padding samples the same finite-record Fourier function more densely; it does not change the identity.  If $N$ samples are padded to length $L$, multiply a standard $1/L$-normalized IDFT by $L/N$ to preserve the $1/N$ normalization.

Using only the nearest transform bin introduces the usual scalloping loss.  For a rectangular window with $N=64$, the loss is approximately $3.92$ dB at half-bin offset.  This is an implementation effect, not a restriction on the identity.

\section*{What the Identity Does and Does Not Say}

Equation~\eqref{eq:chain} holds for every finite vector $\vect{x}$.  Interpreting the finite average as an ensemble cyclic mean is a separate statistical step and requires the usual convergence and phase-reference assumptions.  For example, a tone may have fixed phase within each record but random phase between records; each record then has a nonzero projection even though complex averaging across records can tend to zero.  This affects the interpretation, not the identity.

First-order cyclostationarity describes periodic components of the mean~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.  Higher-order cyclic statistics Fourier-analyze products of samples and can therefore remain nonzero when the cyclic mean vanishes~\cite[eqs. (8.1)--(8.3)]{gardner2006}.  The first-order identity does not collapse that structure.

\section*{Why the Connection Is Useful}

For a carrier, pilot, residual line, or other first-order component, the selected cyclic-mean coefficient can be computed with a tone correlator, a direct finite Fourier evaluation, an IDFT, or an ordinary DFT with reversed bin labels.  The translation brings familiar windowing, leakage, zero-padding, and frequency-refinement tools with it.  It also provides an exact implementation benchmark: disagreement between the cyclic and Fourier forms indicates a sign, conjugation, or normalization error.

The cyclic-mean/DFT relation, in the equivalent unstarred convention, has appeared in harmonic-estimation work~\cite{ghogho1999}.  Writing its full translation to a tone matched-filter output explicitly makes the first-order case accessible and exposes sign, conjugation, and normalization errors.

\section*{Conclusion}

At first order, the selected reduced-dimension cyclic moment is a cyclic-mean coefficient.  For the rectangular $N$-sample record and the complex-correlation configuration used here,
\begin{equation*}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\left[\frac{X_N(-\alpha)}{N}\right]^*
=\left[\frac{y_{\mathrm{MF}}(-\alpha)}{N}\right]^*.
\end{equation*}
In words, the first-order cyclic moment at cycle frequency $\alpha$ is the conjugate of the normalized tone matched-filter output at frequency $-\alpha$.  This identity holds at every evaluated frequency.  The $N$-point IDFT relation is only a convenient grid-specific implementation of the same projection.
