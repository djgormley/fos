\section*{}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{yclostationary} signal processing and classical detection theory often use different terms for the same operation.  The first literature calls it estimation of a first-order cyclic moment.  The second calls it correlation with a complex sinusoid, a Fourier coefficient, or a tone matched-filter output.  For a finite data record, these operations evaluate the same inner product.  In this work, we develop this identity and then show that, for a known-frequency deterministic tone in proper complex white Gaussian noise of known variance, the covariance-normalized first-order cyclic-mean test is equivalent up to a positive scale factor to the quadrature matched-filter generalized likelihood-ratio test (GLRT).  Lastly, we state the limitations of this result.  A discrete Fourier transform (DFT) coefficient is exact only on the DFT grid, colored noise requires whitening, a real sinusoid spans two quadratures, and a random ensemble phase can remove the first-order cyclic mean without removing finite-record tone energy.

\section*{Background}

Wide-sense cyclostationarity is commonly described using periodic second-order moments and spectral correlation.  For a complex time series, the conjugate and nonconjugate autocorrelations provide the relevant second-order coefficients.  Lag-product processing then converts their periodic components into spectral lines~\cite[pp. 646--649 and 663]{gardner2006}.  Higher-order cyclic moments and cumulants extend this construction beyond second order~\cite[pp. 667--669]{gardner2006}.

First-order cyclostationarity is simpler.  Gardner shows that a finite additive sinusoid in a real time series is equivalent to a nonzero time-average Fourier coefficient.  He calls this property first-order periodicity and develops the finite-window exponential extractor~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3) and (17)--(21)]{gardner1986}.  Dobre, Rajan, and Inkol later apply first-order cyclostationarity to joint detection and classification~\cite[pp. 4--5]{dobre2009}.  They define the estimator in Eq. (9)~\cite[eq. (9)]{dobre2009} and observe that it ``practically represents'' the DFT of the data~\cite[p. 7]{dobre2009}.  In detection theory, Kay derives the unknown-amplitude-and-phase GLRT for a complex deterministic tone as a periodogram, or quadrature matched filter~\cite[eqs. (13.49)--(13.53)]{kay1998}.  Spooner and Nicholls similarly describe tone detection as a matched-filter detector implemented using matched filtering or Fourier analysis~\cite[p. 600]{spooner2009}.

These references provide the individual relationships between the cyclic mean, Fourier coefficient, and matched-filter output.  However, none of the reviewed sources states the complete normalized detector identity.  Our contribution is to combine and clarify the existing results, rather than claim the algebra as new:

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{deepblue}{warmgray}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\textbf{Central claim.} For one cycle frequency, the finite-record first-order cyclic-mean estimate is a normalized complex-sinusoid matched-filter output.  In proper complex white Gaussian noise of known variance, its covariance-normalized magnitude square produces the same rejection regions as the unknown-complex-amplitude tone GLRT.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

This claim is intentionally limited.  It does not apply to every first-order procedure or every tone detector.  Moreover, the term ``optimal'' is meaningful only after we specify the signal and noise model.

\section*{The First-Order Cyclic Mean}

We begin with the general $p$th-order cyclic temporal cross-moment~\cite[eq. (13.1)]{gardner2006}:
\begin{equation}
R_{x,p}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=
\left\langle
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}
x_{\ell}^{(*)_{\ell}}(t+\tau_{\ell})
e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}
\right\rangle_t ,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
Here, $p$ is the moment order, $(*)_{\ell}$ specifies whether the corresponding factor is conjugated, $\vect{\tau}$ contains the lag values, and $\langle\cdot\rangle_t$ denotes a time average.  The first-order moment therefore has $p=1$.  Its two conjugation forms are related by
\begin{equation*}
\left\langle x^*(t+\tau)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=
\left[
\left\langle x(t+\tau)e^{+j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
\right]^* .
\end{equation*}
Thus, over a two-sided cycle-frequency domain, the two forms contain the same information: conjugation is paired with the reversal $\alpha\mapsto-\alpha$.  For real-valued $x$, they coincide at each cycle frequency.  No additional conjugation choice is required: specifying the unconjugated coefficient over both signs of $\alpha$ determines the conjugated coefficient completely through the displayed symmetry.  More generally, a $p$th-order moment has only $p-1$ independent relative lags; the reduced-dimension form fixes one reference delay at zero~\cite[eq. (13.7)]{gardner2006}.  At first order, there is no relative-lag coordinate; the only displayed delay is a common time shift.  Because the infinite-time average is invariant to a finite shift, changing variables to $u=t+\tau$ gives
\begin{equation*}
R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(\tau)
=\left\langle x(t+\tau)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=e^{j2\pi\alpha\tau}R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0).
\end{equation*}
This common shift changes the coefficient only by the known phase $e^{j2\pi\alpha\tau}$.  We therefore adopt the reduced-dimension convention $\tau=0$ without loss of information and write the first-order cyclic mean as
\begin{equation}
M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\left\langle x(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\label{eq:cyclicmeanct}
\end{equation}

An ordinary finite-record sample mean is a zero-frequency extractor, so a component at nonzero cycle frequency is generally attenuated by phase cancellation.  To estimate the cyclic mean at a candidate cycle frequency, we remove the corresponding trial rotation.  After sampling, let $\alpha$ denote that candidate frequency in cycles per sample and define the phase-corrected samples $z_{\alpha}[n]\equiv x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}$.  The counter-rotating factor moves a matching component to zero frequency, so the finite-record cyclic-mean estimate is simply the ordinary sample mean of the corrected sequence:
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z_{\alpha}[n]
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\label{eq:cyclicmean}
\end{equation}
For example, if $x[n]=ae^{j2\pi\alpha n}$, then $z_{\alpha}[n]=a$ for every $n$ and $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=a$.  A mismatched trial frequency leaves a residual phase progression and therefore partial cancellation under the average.

Writing these same phase-correction weights as vectors reveals the Fourier and matched-filter interpretations.  Collect the record and the candidate phase progression in
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vect{x}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
x[0] & x[1] & \cdots & x[N-1]
\end{bmatrix}^{T},\\
\vect{s}_{\alpha}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & e^{j2\pi\alpha} & \cdots & e^{j2\pi\alpha(N-1)}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The entries of $\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}$ are exactly the phase-correction factors in~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean}, so
\begin{equation}
N\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}.
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:identity} makes the connection explicit: phase correction followed by summation is correlation with a length-$N$ complex sinusoid, while division by $N$ converts that correlation to a finite-record average.  Under the white-noise model used below, the unnormalized correlation is the corresponding matched-filter output.  The unnormalized sum $N\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ is also the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) of the length-$N$ record evaluated at $\alpha$.  For an integer $k$, $N\widehat M_x^{k/N}=X[k]$, where
\begin{equation}
X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}.
\end{equation}
For arbitrary $\alpha$, the sum remains an exact DTFT evaluation, but it is not generally an $N$-point DFT coefficient.  An FFT-based implementation may use a denser grid, interpolation, or frequency refinement.

\section*{Detector Equivalence}

The Fourier identity alone does not establish optimality.  To do so, we begin with the one-column complex linear model~\cite[Sec. 13.4.1]{kay1998}:
\begin{align}
\mathcal H_0 &: \vect{x}=\vect{w},\nonumber\\
\mathcal H_1 &: \vect{x}=a\vect{s}_{\alpha}+\vect{w},
\qquad
\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\sigma^2\vect{I}),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
Here, the normalized frequency $\alpha$ and noise variance $\sigma^2$ are known, the deterministic complex amplitude $a$ is unknown, and the noise is proper complex white Gaussian.  Apart from constants, the log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\ell(a)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
\left\|\vect{x}-a\vect{s}_{\alpha}\right\|^2 .
\end{equation}
We complete the square and use $\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}=N$ to obtain the maximum-likelihood estimate
\begin{equation}
\widehat a
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\widehat M_x^{\alpha},
\label{eq:ahat}
\end{equation}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:ahat} into the likelihood produces a GLRT that is monotone in
\begin{equation}
T_{\alpha}
=\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}
{\sigma^2\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\frac{N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}.
\label{eq:glrt}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:glrt} provides an exact finite-sample result.  Under~\eqref{eq:model}, the magnitude-squared first-order cyclic-mean detector at one cycle frequency, the periodogram tone detector, and the quadrature matched-filter GLRT produce identical rejection regions without an asymptotic approximation.

We obtain the same conclusion from the covariance-normalized form used for statistical cyclic-feature tests~\cite[eqs. (B.1)--(B.3)]{dobre2009}.  Let
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\vect{m}}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{Re}\{\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\} &
\operatorname{Im}\{\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{equation}
Under $\mathcal H_0$,
$\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{\vect{m}})=\sigma^2\vect{I}_2/(2N)$, so
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\vect{m}}^{T}
\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{\vect{m}})^{-1}
\widehat{\vect{m}}
=\frac{2N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}
=2T_{\alpha}.
\end{equation}
The positive factor of two changes only the threshold scale.  For one channel, Kay's complex-envelope sinusoid GLRT uses the same statistic with the corresponding factor-of-two threshold convention~\cite[eqs. (13.49)--(13.53)]{kay1998}.

We next determine the threshold from the null distribution.  Using the complex-noise convention in~\eqref{eq:model}, $T_{\alpha}$ has a unit-mean exponential distribution under $\mathcal H_0$.  Therefore,
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{FA}}=e^{-\gamma},
\qquad
\gamma=-\log P_{\mathrm{FA}}.
\label{eq:pfa}
\end{equation}
Under $\mathcal H_1$,
\begin{equation}
2T_{\alpha}\sim
\chi'^2_2\!\left(\frac{2N|a|^2}{\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:noncentral}
\end{equation}
which gives the standard noncoherent matched-filter detection probability.  Therefore, classical tone-detection performance expressions can be applied directly to this first-order cyclic test.

If $a$, including its phase, is known, the Neyman-Pearson statistic is instead the coherent quantity
$\operatorname{Re}\{a^*\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}\}$.  The cyclic mean remains the sufficient complex matched-filter output.  However, the scalar decision statistic changes with the nuisance-parameter model.

\section*{Numerical Verification and Grid Mismatch}

We first verify the common statistic using a length-$64$ complex tone in white noise.  The simulation computes both normalized forms of the first-order cyclic and matched-filter statistic, with a maximum absolute difference below $5\times10^{-12}$.  Following this direct check, we perform a Monte Carlo simulation using the common statistic.  As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(a), the detection probability follows the noncentral chi-square law derived above.

Next, we restrict the detector to a single DFT bin.  This bin is matched only to its center frequency.  For a rectangular length-$N$ record, a half-bin tone offset reduces the single-bin output by approximately $3.92$ dB, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(b).  In comparison, the first-order cyclic estimate at the true $\alpha$ remains exactly tuned.  Therefore, when $\alpha$ is not on the DFT grid, a fast Fourier transform (FFT) implementation requires a denser grid, interpolation, or a frequency-refinement stage.

\section*{Limitations}

The detector equivalence depends on the frequency, noise, signal, and averaging assumptions.  We now consider how the implementation changes when these assumptions are relaxed.

\textbf{Unknown frequency.}
If the frequency is unknown, we maximize~\eqref{eq:glrt} over a candidate set:
\begin{equation}
T=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal A}
\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}{N\sigma^2}.
\end{equation}
This operation is a bank of tone matched filters, also known as a peak-periodogram detector.  It is an FFT-bin search only when $\mathcal A$ is the DFT grid.  The false-alarm threshold must also account for the search; Equation~\eqref{eq:pfa} assumes that one preassigned trial frequency is tested.

\textbf{Colored noise.}
For colored noise with known positive-definite covariance $\vect{C}$ and $\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\vect{C})$, the GLRT becomes
\begin{equation}
T_C=
\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{x}|^2}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}.
\label{eq:colored}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:colored} is the matched filter after whitening.  Therefore, the raw cyclic mean or DFT coefficient is generally not optimal in colored noise.  The unwhitened statistic remains optimal when $\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{s}_{\alpha}$ is proportional to $\vect{s}_{\alpha}$, including for white noise and some idealized circulant covariance models.

\textbf{Real tones.}
A real sinusoid contains positive- and negative-frequency components.  When its amplitude and phase are unknown, detection projects onto the sine-cosine subspace.  At a non-DC, non-Nyquist DFT bin, these quadratures are orthogonal and the statistic reduces to a scaled $|X[k]|^2$.  Off grid, however, we must retain the Gram-matrix normalization rather than assume it is diagonal.

\textbf{Multiple harmonics.}
A general periodic mean may contain several Fourier components.  Testing its complete first-order signature is then a matched-subspace problem rather than a single matched filter.  For distinct DFT-bin harmonics in white noise, we sum the normalized bin energies.  The single-$\alpha$ identity still applies to each component.

\textbf{Windowing.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean} assumes a rectangular observation interval.  A tapered cyclic estimate remains a windowed sinusoidal correlator, but it is not the white-noise matched filter for a constant-envelope, rectangularly observed tone unless the taper represents the true signal envelope.  Thus, tapering trades coherent signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for reduced leakage.

\textbf{Random ensemble phase.}
Lastly, we consider what is meant by ``first-order cyclostationarity.''  Let
\begin{equation}
X[n]=Ae^{j(2\pi\alpha_0 n+\Phi)}+W[n],
\qquad \Phi\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi),
\label{eq:randomphase}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is drawn once and held constant within each realization.  Ensemble averaging gives $\E\{X[n]\}=0$, so the ensemble first-order cyclic mean is zero.  If we condition on the realized phase and ignore noise, however,
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}=Ae^{j\Phi},
\end{equation}
and $|\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}|^2$ remains detectable.  Complex averaging across independently phase-randomized records can therefore cancel the tone, while noncoherent magnitude-square combining does not.

The assumption-free identity is between the \emph{finite-record cyclic-mean estimate} and the tone correlator.  Equating tone presence with a nonzero \emph{ensemble} first-order cyclic mean additionally requires a phase reference or an appropriate first-order cycloergodicity condition.

\section*{Relationship to Higher-Order Cyclostationarity}

The emphasis on second- and higher-order cyclostationarity does not make the first-order result new.  First-order processing identifies a periodic mean, or spectral line~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.  Second-order processing forms lag products and converts their periodic components into spectral lines.  As a result, it exposes periodic covariance even when the ordinary mean is zero~\cite[eqs. (8.1)--(8.3)]{gardner2006}.  Broader cyclic-statistics frameworks develop the corresponding estimation and testing methods, including higher-order extensions~\cite[Secs. 4.1, 11, and 13.2]{gardner2006}.

The first-order case connects three descriptions of the same quantity.  Cyclostationary terminology emphasizes the Fourier coefficient of the time-varying mean.  Fourier terminology emphasizes the resulting spectral line.  Detection terminology emphasizes correlation with a known waveform.  Under the stated model, all three descriptions produce the same sufficient statistic.

\section*{Practical Considerations}

\begin{itemize}
\item A first-order cyclic estimator at one cycle frequency does not require a specialized implementation.  We can use a complex mixer and accumulator, a DTFT evaluation, or an FFT bin when the frequency is on grid.
\item Matched-filter threshold and performance results can be reused after the normalizations are aligned.  For the proper complex white-noise model with known $\sigma^2$ used here, $N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2/\sigma^2$ has a unit-mean exponential distribution under the null hypothesis.
\item A DFT coefficient and first-order cyclic estimate are rescaled forms of the same correlation when they use the same record, frequency, window, and normalization.  Calling that correlation a matched-filter detector additionally requires the windowed sinusoid to be the signal template.
\item The implementation must change when the assumptions change.  We search or interpolate when the frequency is unknown, whiten the data when the noise is colored, retain two real quadratures for a real tone, and use a subspace statistic for multiple harmonics.
\item A nonzero finite-record tone statistic does not necessarily imply a nonzero ensemble cyclic mean.  Therefore, the phase-coherence and averaging assumptions must be stated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Conclusion}

We investigated the relationship between a first-order cyclic-mean detector, a Fourier coefficient, and a tone matched filter.  We first reduced the general cyclic moment to its first-order form.  We then showed that the finite-record estimate is a normalized complex-sinusoid correlation.  Following this, we developed the GLRT for a known-frequency complex tone with unknown amplitude in proper complex white Gaussian noise of known variance.  Under this model, the covariance-normalized cyclic-mean test, periodogram detector, and quadrature matched-filter GLRT are the same statistic up to scale.

This equivalence allows matched-filter thresholds and performance expressions to be applied directly to the first-order cyclic detector.  However, the implementation changes with the model.  An unknown frequency requires a search, colored noise requires whitening, real tones require two quadratures, and multiple harmonics require a matched-subspace statistic.  A finite-record tone can also remain detectable when ensemble phase randomization forces the first-order cyclic mean to zero.  Thus, the result helps us identify the correct detector and its limitations for a given signal model.
