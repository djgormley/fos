\section*{}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{yclostationarity} is usually introduced through second- and higher-order statistics, so first order is easy to overlook.  At first order, the conventional cyclic moment is the cyclic mean: conjugate the record, counter-rotate, and average.  The complex conjugate of this output is a normalized inverse-transform evaluation and a $1/N$-normalized correlation with a complex tone at the opposite frequency; on the inverse discrete Fourier transform (IDFT) grid, it is an IDFT output.  Under the white-Gaussian tone model stated below, its noise-normalized energy is also a generalized likelihood-ratio test (GLRT) statistic for an unknown complex amplitude.

This is not a new detector.  The useful point is that a first-order cyclic feature can be implemented and analyzed with familiar tone-detection tools.  We begin with the first-order reduction, then follow the cyclic mean's complex conjugate through its inverse-Fourier, matched-filter, and GLRT interpretations.

\section*{Why First Order Is the Cyclic Mean}

A $p$th-order cyclic moment averages a product of $p$ possibly conjugated and shifted signal factors after counter-rotation.  At $p=1$, only one factor remains and there are no relative lags.  The reduction is easiest to see from the single-signal form of the continuous-time definition~\cite[eq. (13.1)]{gardner2006}:
\begin{equation}
R_{x,p}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=
\left\langle
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}
x^{(*)_{\ell}}(t+\tau_{\ell})
e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}
\right\rangle_t ,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
Here, $(*)_{\ell}$ denotes optional conjugation and $\langle\cdot\rangle_t$ denotes a time average.  The full form uses $p$ delays, whereas the reduced form uses $p-1$ relative lags.  Setting $p=1$ leaves one common reference delay.  We choose the complex-correlation configuration in which the remaining factor is $x^*$ and denote its first-order coefficient by $M_x^\alpha$.  Shift invariance of the infinite-time average gives
\begin{equation}
R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(\tau)
=\left\langle x^*(t+\tau)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=e^{j2\pi\alpha\tau}R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0).
\label{eq:firstorderlag}
\end{equation}
The delay $\tau$ shifts the only factor rather than measuring separation between factors.  Equation~\eqref{eq:firstorderlag} shows that it changes only a known phase.  The reduced-dimension convention therefore sets $\tau=0$ without losing information~\cite[eq. (13.7)]{gardner2006}:
\begin{equation}
M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\left\langle x^*(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\label{eq:firstorderzero}
\end{equation}
With no relative lag, there is also no lag variable to Fourier transform.  Equation~\eqref{eq:firstorderzero} defines the cyclic-mean notation used here.

To connect this coefficient to a Fourier operation on the original record $x$, conjugate the completed average:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left[M_x^{\alpha}\right]^*
&=\left[R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)\right]^*\\
&=\left\langle x(t)e^{+j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:inversesign}
\end{equation}
The positive exponential is the inverse-transform sign.  Thus, a conventional cyclic coefficient at cycle frequency $\alpha$ corresponds to a tone at frequency $-\alpha$ in the original record.

For a sampled finite record, estimate the cyclic mean directly with the $N$-sample average below.  Treating that finite-record average as an estimate of the ensemble cyclic mean also assumes a shared phase reference and convergence of the time average to the ensemble coefficient.

\section*{The Core Operation: Conjugate, Counter-Rotate, Then Average}

Consider the complex record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, and let $\alpha$ be the cycle frequency in cycles per sample, understood modulo one.  An ordinary mean extracts zero frequency, so a nonzero-frequency component tends to cancel as its phase rotates.  Conjugation followed by counter-rotation moves a candidate component at $-\alpha$ to zero frequency:
\begin{equation*}
z_{\alpha}[n]\equiv x^*[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\end{equation*}
For the unweighted rectangular observation used here, the cyclic-mean estimate is the ordinary mean of the corrected samples:
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z_{\alpha}[n]
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^*[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\label{eq:cyclicmean}
\end{equation}
Here, $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ is the finite-record estimate, and the decision statistic $T_{-\alpha}$ is defined below.  For $x[n]=ae^{-j2\pi\alpha n}$, every corrected sample equals $a^*$, so $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=a^*$.  At a mismatched frequency, the corrected samples still rotate and partially cancel.

\textbf{Inverse-Fourier and correlator views.}
Writing the phase-correction weights as vectors makes the connection explicit.  Collect the record and the matching negative-frequency tone in
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vect{x}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
x[0] & x[1] & \cdots & x[N-1]
\end{bmatrix}^{T},\\
\vect{s}_{-\alpha}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & e^{-j2\pi\alpha} & \cdots & e^{-j2\pi\alpha(N-1)}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Conjugating~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean} exposes the positive-exponent weights in $\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}$.  Define the unnormalized inverse-sign finite Fourier evaluation
\begin{equation*}
\widetilde X_N(\alpha)
\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{+j2\pi\alpha n}.
\end{equation*}
Equivalently, if $X_N(f)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi fn}$ denotes the usual finite-record Fourier evaluation, then $\widetilde X_N(\alpha)=X_N(-\alpha)$.
The four views are therefore
\begin{equation}
\left[\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\right]^*
=\left[\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)\right]^*
=\frac{\widetilde X_N(\alpha)}{N}
=\frac{\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}{N}.
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
Write $X[k]\equiv X_N(k/N)$ for the ordinary $N$-point DFT.  On the IDFT grid, $\alpha\equiv k/N\pmod 1$, so
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{IDFT}_N\{\vect{x}\}[k]
\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{+j2\pi kn/N}
=\frac{X[(-k)\bmod N]}{N}
=\left[\widehat M_x^{k/N}\right]^*.
\end{equation}
Off grid, $\widetilde X_N(\alpha)$ is still an exact inverse-sign Fourier evaluation rather than an $N$-point IDFT output.  Zero padding supplies exact evaluations on a denser grid, while interpolation or refinement estimates an arbitrary off-grid location.  If $N$ samples are padded to length $L$ and a normalized $L$-point IDFT is used, multiply its output by $L/N$ to preserve the normalization in~\eqref{eq:chain}.  This is the usual transform-grid issue, not a limitation of the first-order identity.  Conjugating Gardner's time-average Fourier coefficient, or equivalently reversing its frequency, gives the corresponding infinite-record view~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.

\section*{When the Common Output Is a GLRT}

None of the identities above requires a noise model.  The GLRT interpretation requires the following signal and noise model~\cite[Sec. 13.4.1]{kay1998}:
\begin{align}
\mathcal H_0 &: \vect{x}=\vect{w},\nonumber\\
\mathcal H_1 &: \vect{x}=a\vect{s}_{-\alpha}+\vect{w},
\qquad
\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\sigma^2\vect{I}),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
Here, the tone frequency $-\alpha$ and the noise variance $\sigma^2$ are known, while the deterministic complex amplitude $a$ is unknown.  The noise is proper complex white Gaussian.  Apart from constants, the log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\ell(a)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
\left\|\vect{x}-a\vect{s}_{-\alpha}\right\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Because $\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{-\alpha}=N$, maximizing the likelihood gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat a
=\frac{\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}
{\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{-\alpha}}
=\left[\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\right]^*
=\left[\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)\right]^*,
\label{eq:ahat}
\end{equation}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:ahat} gives a GLRT that is monotone in
\begin{equation}
T_{-\alpha}
=\frac{|\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}
{\sigma^2\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{-\alpha}}
=\frac{N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}
=\frac{N|\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)|^2}{\sigma^2}.
\label{eq:glrt}
\end{equation}

This result is exact for any finite $N$.  Under~\eqref{eq:model}, the same number is noise-normalized cyclic-mean energy and the usual noncoherent GLRT statistic for a tone.  This tone-detection connection is also developed from matched-filter and Fourier viewpoints in~\cite[p. 600]{spooner2009}.

Under $\mathcal H_0$, $T_{-\alpha}$ has a unit-mean exponential distribution, so
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{FA}}=e^{-\gamma},
\qquad
\gamma=-\log P_{\mathrm{FA}}.
\label{eq:pfa}
\end{equation}
\begin{samepage}
The direct implementation has three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Correlate and sum: $u_{-\alpha}=\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}$.
\item Normalize: $T_{-\alpha}=|u_{-\alpha}|^2/(N\sigma^2)$.
\item Threshold: decide $\mathcal H_1$ when $T_{-\alpha}>\gamma$.
\end{enumerate}
This threshold is exact for one fixed $\alpha$ and known $\sigma^2$.  Estimating $\sigma^2$ changes the null distribution and threshold.
\end{samepage}
Under $\mathcal H_1$,
\begin{equation}
2T_{-\alpha}\sim
\chi'^2_2\!\left(\frac{2N|a|^2}{\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:noncentral}
\end{equation}
which gives the standard noncoherent matched-filter detection probability.  Classical tone-detection performance expressions therefore apply directly to $T_{-\alpha}$.

\section*{Numerical Verification and Grid Mismatch}

For a length-$64$ complex tone at frequency $-\alpha$ in white noise, computing $T_{-\alpha}$ from the cyclic mean at $\alpha$ or from the matched filter gives the same result to numerical precision.  Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(a) compares the simulated detection probability with the predicted noncentral chi-square law at $P_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.01$, using per-sample SNR $\rho=|a|^2/\sigma^2$.  The repository script reproduces both panels and is available at \url{https://github.com/djgormley/fos}.

Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(b) shows the familiar transform-grid mismatch.  For the rectangular $N=64$ record, a tone halfway between two bins retains the exact power fraction $[N\sin(\pi/(2N))]^{-2}$, a $3.92$-dB loss when evaluated at one bin.  Evaluating the cyclic mean at the negative of the tone's true frequency has no mismatch loss.  Zero padding, interpolation, or frequency refinement provides the same remedies used in higher-order cyclic-spectrum processing.

\section*{Where the Equivalence Needs Care}

The finite-record identity remains exact; the following points affect how its output is used or interpreted.

\textbf{Unknown frequency.}
If the frequency is unknown, maximize~\eqref{eq:glrt} over a candidate set.  This gives a bank of tone matched filters, or a peak-periodogram detector; an inverse FFT evaluates the search on the IDFT grid, equivalently an ordinary FFT with reversed bin labels.  Because several frequencies are tested, the single-frequency threshold in~\eqref{eq:pfa} no longer applies.

\textbf{Colored noise.}
For proper complex $\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\vect{C})$ with known positive-definite covariance $\vect{C}$, whitening the record and tone template gives
\begin{equation}
T_{C,-\alpha}=
\frac{|\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{x}|^2}
{\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{s}_{-\alpha}}.
\label{eq:colored}
\end{equation}
The unweighted cyclic-mean or inverse-sign Fourier output remains algebraically equivalent to the tone correlation but is generally not the optimal detector.

\textbf{Random ensemble phase.}
A finite record can contain a detectable tone even when its ensemble cyclic mean is zero.  Let
\begin{equation}
X[n]=Ae^{j(-2\pi\alpha_0 n+\Phi)}+W[n],
\qquad \Phi\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi),
\label{eq:randomphase}
\end{equation}
where $A\geq0$ is deterministic, $W[n]$ is zero mean, and $\Phi$ is drawn independently for each realization but held fixed within it.  Because $\E\{e^{j\Phi}\}=0$, we have $\E\{X[n]\}=0$.  Within one realization, however, the phase is fixed and, ignoring noise,
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}
=\widehat R_{X,1}^{\alpha_0}(0)
=Ae^{-j\Phi},
\end{equation}
and $|\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}|^2$ remains detectable.  Thus, complex averaging across independently phased records tends to zero, whereas averaging squared magnitudes preserves the tone energy.

\section*{Why the Connection Is Useful}

Second- and higher-order cyclic statistics can reveal periodic dependence when the ordinary mean is zero~\cite[eqs. (8.1)--(8.3)]{gardner2006}.  First order instead describes spectral-line components in the signal, or in its mean under the stochastic formulation~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.  The equivalent sign-and-conjugation convention has been used to detect and classify AM and FSK signals~\cite{dobre2009}.

For a carrier, pilot, residual line, or other first-order component, the identity permits an inverse FFT, an ordinary FFT with reversed bin labels, or a tone correlator and brings standard mismatch analysis, thresholds, and performance expressions with it.  It also provides an exact implementation benchmark.  A missed feature then points to familiar causes---frequency mismatch, colored noise, or phase incoherence; independent phases may call for magnitude-square combining or a higher-order cyclic statistic.

\section*{Conclusion}

At first order, the conventional reduced-dimension cyclic moment is the cyclic mean.  For a rectangular $N$-sample record,
\begin{equation*}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\left[\frac{\widetilde X_N(\alpha)}{N}\right]^*
=\left[\frac{\vect{s}_{-\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}{N}\right]^*.
\end{equation*}
In words, the cyclic mean at cycle frequency $\alpha$ is the conventional first-order moment, and its complex conjugate is the normalized inverse-transform output and tone correlation at frequency $-\alpha$.

\begin{samepage}
Under the model in~\eqref{eq:model}, the normalized energy of this common output is the matched-filter GLRT statistic:
\begin{equation*}
\boxed{
T_{-\alpha}
=\frac{N|\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)|^2}{\sigma^2}.
}
\end{equation*}
\end{samepage}
Thus, the tone matched-filter GLRT statistic at frequency $-\alpha$ is the conventional first-order cyclic-moment energy at cycle frequency $\alpha$, measured in noise units.  This result holds at every evaluated $\alpha$; only the $N$-point IDFT shortcut requires $\alpha\equiv k/N\pmod 1$.  Equivalently, $|\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)|=(\sigma/\sqrt{N})\sqrt{T_{-\alpha}}$, so $T_{-\alpha}$ determines the estimate's magnitude but not its phase.

The practical lesson is simple: under the stated model, first-order cyclic detection at cycle frequency $\alpha$ is ordinary tone detection at frequency $-\alpha$ written in cyclic-statistics notation.
