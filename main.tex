\noindent Cyclostationarity is usually introduced through second- and higher-order statistics, so its first-order case is easy to overlook.  Yet setting $p=1$ in the general cyclic-moment estimator leaves one signal factor multiplied by a phase correction.  The two optional-star configurations are the cyclic means of $x$ and $x^*$, denoted $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ and $\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}$.  They are conjugate reflections and map symmetrically to a normalized tone matched-filter output.  These identities need no noise model or frequency grid.

Classical theory identifies the cyclic mean with the coefficient of a finite-strength additive sine wave and derives it through an individual harmonic-selecting filter~\cite[eqs. (17)--(21)]{gardner1986}.  Harmonic-estimation work likewise identifies the data DFT as the cyclic mean and relates DFT peak picking to fitting a constant-amplitude harmonic~\cite[Sec. 1]{ghogho1999}.  The pedagogical point here is to make the exact finite-record tone matched-filter identity explicit, including its normalization and the conjugate-reflected optional-star branch.

\section*{Endpoint 1: The Cyclic Estimate}

Consider a complex record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, with cycle frequency $\alpha$ measured in cycles per sample and integer sample delays $\vect{\tau}=(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_p)$.  Following Gardner et al., let $x^{(*)_\ell}$ mean that the $\ell$th factor is either $x$ or $x^*$, and let $\mathcal{C}=((*)_1,\ldots,(*)_p)$ denote the selected conjugation configuration.  Replacing the time average in Gardner's reduced-dimension order-$p$ cyclic moment~\cite[eqs. (13.21) and (13.25)]{gardner2006} with an equally weighted average over the available finite record gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat R_{x,p,\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=\frac{1}{N_{\vect{\tau}}}
\sum_{n\in\mathcal I_{\vect{\tau}}}
\left[
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}x^{(*)_\ell}[n+\tau_\ell]
\right]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n},
\qquad \tau_p=0,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal I_{\vect{\tau}}$ contains the indices for which every shifted sample is available and $N_{\vect{\tau}}=|\mathcal I_{\vect{\tau}}|$.  The exponential is the phase-correction, or counter-rotation, factor: it removes the cycle phase at $\alpha$ before the product is averaged.

Equation~\eqref{eq:general} uses a fixed reference: $\tau_p=0$ places the final factor at $n$, and the other delays are measured relative to it.  The optional star changes a sample's complex value, not its time argument.

At second order, the product is $x[n+\tau]x^{(*)}[n]$~\cite[eq. (3.67)]{gardner2006}; selecting the star does not send $\tau$ to $-\tau$.  A second-order lag changes sign only when the two time arguments are exchanged.  Because correlation labels and subscript conventions vary~\cite[p. 650]{gardner2006}, below we name the first-order branches by the factor that remains.

\section*{Set $p=1$: The Two Cyclic Means}

At $p=1$, the product in~\eqref{eq:general} contains one factor, so no relative lag remains; $(0)$ records the fixed reference.  In the underlying time average, moving that reference to $\tau$ multiplies the coefficient by $\ee^{\jj2\pi\alpha\tau}$ for either factor choice.  Zero therefore fixes the time-origin phase before the finite record is taken.

We show the selected factor directly in the subscript and define the two first-order cyclic means in parallel:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
&\equiv\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n},\\
\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}
&\equiv\widehat R_{x^*,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x^*[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:twocyclicmeans}
\end{equation}
The first line is the equally weighted finite-record counterpart of Gardner's cyclic mean~\cite[eq. (2)]{gardner1986}; the second applies the same definition to $x^*$.  Thus the star in $\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}$ belongs to the input sequence, not to the completed coefficient.

Before introducing a matched filter, compare the two cyclic means directly.  Conjugating the completed $x^*$ coefficient gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left[\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}\right]^*
&=\left[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x^*[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n}
\right]^*\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x[n]\ee^{+\jj2\pi\alpha n}\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi(-\alpha)n}
=\widehat M_x^{-\alpha}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:firstorderreflection}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:firstorderreflection} shows that, once the completed $x^*$ coefficient is conjugated, the only remaining difference is the negative sign on the cycle frequency.  It is a frequency reflection, not a lag reversal; no relative lag remains at first order.

\section*{Endpoint 2: The Tone Matched Filter}

For a unit-amplitude tone at frequency $f$ cycles per sample, define
\begin{equation}
s_f[n]=\ee^{\jj2\pi fn},\qquad 0\leq n<N.
\label{eq:tone}
\end{equation}
The standard matched filter uses the conjugated, time-reversed template~\cite[eqs. (5.28)--(5.29)]{lanterman2016}.  Its discrete-time, causally delayed counterpart places full overlap at output index $N-1$:
\begin{equation}
h_f[m]=s_f^*[N-1-m],\qquad 0\leq m<N.
\label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
At full alignment, its output is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)
&\equiv (x*h_f)[N-1]\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]s_f^*[n]
=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi fn}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matchedfilter}
\end{equation}
The equality derived below is deterministic; the usual claim that this particular template correlator is SNR-optimal assumes white noise or prior whitening.  Because $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|s_f[n]|^2=N$, dividing $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)$ by $N$ gives the unit-gain projection coefficient.  Nothing in this inner product requires $f$ to be an FFT-bin frequency; an FFT merely evaluates it on its own frequency grid.

\section*{Put the Endpoints Together}

Comparing the first line of~\eqref{eq:twocyclicmeans} with the final sum in~\eqref{eq:matchedfilter} gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^f
=\widehat R_{x,1}^f(0)
=\frac{y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)}{N}.
\label{eq:directmatch}
\end{equation}
Applying~\eqref{eq:firstorderreflection} at $\alpha=-f$ gives the same coefficient from the star-present branch.  Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)
&=N\widehat M_x^f
=N\widehat R_{x,1}^f(0),\\
&=N\left[\widehat M_{x^*}^{-f}\right]^*
=N\left[\widehat R_{x^*,1}^{-f}(0)\right]^*.
\end{aligned}
}
\label{eq:matchedidentities}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{deepblue}{warmgray}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\textbf{The tip.} The two first-order cyclic means satisfy $[\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}]^*=\widehat M_x^{-\alpha}$.  A tone matched filter at $f$ produces $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)=N\widehat M_x^f$, and therefore also $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)=N[\widehat M_{x^*}^{-f}]^*$.  The $x$ path uses $f$ directly; the $x^*$ path uses $-f$ and conjugates the completed coefficient.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

For example, if $x[n]=a\ee^{\jj2\pi\alpha n}$, then $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=a$ and $\widehat M_{x^*}^{-\alpha}=a^*$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:matchedidentities} therefore gives $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(\alpha)=Na$ through the $x$ path directly or through the $x^*$ path after completed conjugation.

\section*{Why Point It Out?}

The identities provide a simple implementation check: disagreement between either cyclic-mean branch and its corresponding equality in~\eqref{eq:matchedidentities} exposes a sign, conjugation, or normalization error.  They also place often-overlooked first-order cyclic quantities in familiar signal-processing language.  A carrier, pilot, or residual line can be handled through either cyclic mean or the corresponding tone correlator because, at first order, they are the same finite-record projection under the stated sign and conjugation convention.

\section*{Conclusion}

At $p=1$, the two optional-star configurations reduce to the conjugate-reflected cyclic means $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ and $\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}$.  Their common matched-filter form is $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)=N\widehat M_x^f=N[\widehat M_{x^*}^{-f}]^*$.  The identity is exact for every finite record and every evaluated $f$.
