\noindent Cyclostationary theory is best known for its second- and higher-order statistics.  Its moment hierarchy, however, begins at order $p=1$.  Stop the hierarchy there and the general cyclic estimator becomes a phase-corrected average.  For a finite record, the star-absent estimate is exactly a normalized tone matched-filter output; the star-present estimate gives the same output after conjugation and frequency reflection.  These identities are deterministic, hold at every evaluated frequency, and give first-order cyclostationarity an immediate interpretation in familiar signal-processing terms.

The individual links are classical.  Gardner identifies the cyclic mean with the coefficient of a finite-strength additive sine wave and derives it through an individual harmonic-selecting filter~\cite[eqs. (17)--(21)]{gardner1986}.  Harmonic-estimation work identifies the data DFT as the cyclic mean and relates DFT peak picking to fitting a constant-amplitude harmonic~\cite[Sec. 1]{ghogho1999}.  Taken together, these treatments supply the ingredients of the finite-record relation.  Here we write that relation directly from the general optional-star cyclic moment, including its normalization and conjugate-reflected branch.  That small synthesis is the point: a familiar tone projection is already present at the first order of the general cyclostationary framework.

\section*{Start with the General Cyclic Moment}

Consider a complex record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, with cycle frequency $\alpha$ measured in cycles per sample and integer sample delays $\vect{\tau}=(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_p)$.  Following Gardner et al., let $x^{(*)_\ell}$ mean that the $\ell$th factor is either $x$ or $x^*$, and let $\mathcal{C}=((*)_1,\ldots,(*)_p)$ denote the selected conjugation configuration.  Replacing the time average in Gardner's reduced-dimension order-$p$ cyclic moment~\cite[eqs. (13.21) and (13.25)]{gardner2006} with an equally weighted average over the available finite record gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat R_{x,p,\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=\frac{1}{N_{\vect{\tau}}}
\sum_{n\in\mathcal I_{\vect{\tau}}}
\left[
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}x^{(*)_\ell}[n+\tau_\ell]
\right]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n},
\qquad \tau_p=0,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal I_{\vect{\tau}}$ contains the indices for which every shifted sample is available and $N_{\vect{\tau}}=|\mathcal I_{\vect{\tau}}|$.  The exponential is the phase-correction, or counter-rotation, factor: it removes the cycle phase at $\alpha$ before the product is averaged.

Gardner's reduced-dimension convention fixes the final factor at zero delay, so the other delays are measured relative to it.  The optional star replaces a selected factor by its complex conjugate without changing its time argument.

\section*{Stop at First Order}

At $p=1$, the product in~\eqref{eq:general} contains only the fixed-reference factor.  There is no relative delay to estimate; the $(0)$ simply records the reference already fixed in~\eqref{eq:general}.  The estimator therefore reduces immediately to two finite-record cyclic means:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
&\equiv\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n},\\
\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}
&\equiv\widehat R_{x^*,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x^*[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:twocyclicmeans}
\end{equation}
The first line is the equally weighted finite-record counterpart of Gardner's cyclic mean~\cite[eq. (2)]{gardner1986}; the second applies the same definition to $x^*$.  The star in $\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}$ therefore belongs to the input sequence, not to the completed coefficient.

Across reflected cycle frequencies, the two branches contain the same information.  Conjugating the completed $x^*$ coefficient gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left[\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}\right]^*
&=\left[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x^*[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi\alpha n}
\right]^*\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x[n]\ee^{+\jj2\pi\alpha n}\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
x[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi(-\alpha)n}
=\widehat M_x^{-\alpha}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:firstorderreflection}
\end{equation}
Thus the completed coefficients are conjugate reflections in cycle frequency.

\section*{Recognize the Tone Matched Filter}

Now compare~\eqref{eq:twocyclicmeans} with the familiar projection onto a tone.  For a unit-amplitude tone at frequency $f$ cycles per sample, define
\begin{equation}
s_f[n]=\ee^{\jj2\pi fn},\qquad 0\leq n<N.
\label{eq:tone}
\end{equation}
The standard matched filter uses the conjugated, time-reversed template~\cite[eqs. (5.28)--(5.29)]{lanterman2016}.  Its discrete-time, causally delayed counterpart places full overlap at output index $N-1$:
\begin{equation}
h_f[m]=s_f^*[N-1-m],\qquad 0\leq m<N.
\label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
At full alignment, its output is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)
&\equiv (x*h_f)[N-1]\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]s_f^*[n]
=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\ee^{-\jj2\pi fn}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matchedfilter}
\end{equation}
Because $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|s_f[n]|^2=N$, dividing $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)$ by $N$ gives the unit-gain projection coefficient.  The algebraic identity below is deterministic.  Interpreting this correlator as SNR-optimal assumes white noise or prior whitening, but the identity itself needs neither a noise model nor an FFT-bin grid; an FFT merely evaluates the projection on its own frequency grid.

\section*{State the Identity}

The decisive observation is that the first line of~\eqref{eq:twocyclicmeans} and the final line of~\eqref{eq:matchedfilter} contain the same finite-record inner product.  Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^f
=\widehat R_{x,1}^f(0)
=\frac{y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)}{N}.
\label{eq:directmatch}
\end{equation}
Applying~\eqref{eq:firstorderreflection} at $\alpha=-f$ gives the same coefficient from the star-present branch.  Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)
&=N\widehat M_x^f
=N\widehat R_{x,1}^f(0),\\
&=N\left[\widehat M_{x^*}^{-f}\right]^*
=N\left[\widehat R_{x^*,1}^{-f}(0)\right]^*.
\end{aligned}
}
\label{eq:matchedidentities}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{deepblue}{warmgray}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\textbf{The tip.} The first-order cyclic-mean estimate $\widehat M_x^f$ is exactly a normalized tone matched-filter output.  Specifically, $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)=N\widehat M_x^f=N[\widehat M_{x^*}^{-f}]^*$, while the two cyclic means satisfy $[\widehat M_{x^*}^{\alpha}]^*=\widehat M_x^{-\alpha}$.  The $x$ path uses $f$ directly; the $x^*$ path uses $-f$ and conjugates the completed coefficient.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

For example, if $x[n]=a\ee^{\jj2\pi\alpha n}$, then $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=a$ and $\widehat M_{x^*}^{-\alpha}=a^*$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:matchedidentities} therefore gives $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(\alpha)=Na$ through the $x$ path directly or through the $x^*$ path after completed conjugation.

\section*{Why the Identity Is Worth Stating}

The value of~\eqref{eq:matchedidentities} is conceptual before it is computational.  Cyclostationary analysis calls the unit-gain projection coefficient a cyclic mean; matched-filter language calls the corresponding unnormalized inner product a tone matched-filter output.  A carrier, pilot, or residual line can therefore be interpreted in either vocabulary.  The factor $N$ separates the normalizations, not the underlying computation.  The identity does not create a new estimator---that is precisely the point.  It identifies the familiar estimator already hiding at first order in the general cyclic-moment hierarchy.  As a secondary benefit, disagreement between the two sides of~\eqref{eq:matchedidentities} exposes a sign, conjugation, or normalization error.

\section*{Conclusion}

Following the general cyclic-moment hierarchy down to $p=1$ reveals a familiar object: the normalized tone matched-filter output.  The two optional-star branches are conjugate reflections, and their common form is $y_{\mathrm{MF}}(f)=N\widehat M_x^f=N[\widehat M_{x^*}^{-f}]^*$.  The ingredients are classical, but writing the complete finite-record relation directly makes the bridge explicit.  It is exact for every finite record and every evaluated $f$.
