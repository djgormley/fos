\section*{}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{yclostationarity} is usually introduced through second- and higher-order statistics, so first order is easy to overlook.  At first order, the cyclic moment is the cyclic mean: counter-rotate the record and average.  The same calculation gives a discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) sample and a correlation with a complex tone, each divided by $N$; on the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) grid, it is $X[k]/N$.  Under the white-Gaussian tone model stated below, its noise-normalized energy is also a generalized likelihood-ratio test (GLRT) statistic for an unknown complex amplitude.

This is not a new detector.  The useful point is that a first-order cyclic feature can be implemented and analyzed with familiar tone-detection tools.  We begin with the first-order reduction, then follow its finite-record estimate through its Fourier, matched-filter, and GLRT interpretations.

\section*{Why First Order Is the Cyclic Mean}

A $p$th-order cyclic moment averages a product of $p$ possibly conjugated and shifted signal factors after counter-rotation.  At $p=1$, only one factor remains and there are no relative lags.  The reduction is easiest to see from the single-signal form of the continuous-time definition~\cite[eq. (13.1)]{gardner2006}:
\begin{equation}
R_{x,p}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=
\left\langle
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}
x^{(*)_{\ell}}(t+\tau_{\ell})
e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}
\right\rangle_t ,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
Here, $(*)_{\ell}$ denotes optional conjugation of the signal factor $x$, and $\langle\cdot\rangle_t$ denotes a time average.  The full form uses $p$ delays, whereas the reduced form uses $p-1$ relative lags.  Setting $p=1$ leaves one common reference delay.  Shift invariance of the infinite-time average gives
\begin{equation}
R_{x,1}^{\alpha,(*)}(\tau)
=\left\langle x^{(*)}(t+\tau)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t
=e^{j2\pi\alpha\tau}R_{x,1}^{\alpha,(*)}(0).
\label{eq:firstorderlag}
\end{equation}
The delay $\tau$ shifts the only factor rather than measuring separation between factors.  Equation~\eqref{eq:firstorderlag} shows that it changes only a known phase.  The reduced-dimension convention therefore sets $\tau=0$ without losing information~\cite[eq. (13.7)]{gardner2006}:
\begin{equation}
R_{x,1}^{\alpha,(*)}(0)
=\left\langle x^{(*)}(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\label{eq:firstorderzero}
\end{equation}
With no relative lag, there is also no lag variable to Fourier transform.

Evaluating the two choices in~\eqref{eq:firstorderzero} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
&=\left\langle x(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t,\\
R_{x,1}^{\alpha,*}(0)
&=\left\langle x^*(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t\\
&=\left[\left\langle x(t)e^{+j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t\right]^*
=\left[R_{x,1}^{-\alpha}(0)\right]^*.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:conjugation}
\end{equation}
The star following $\alpha$ labels conjugation of the signal factor; it does not conjugate the entire coefficient.  Thus, a two-sided evaluation of the unstarred branch supplies the starred branch for free: negate the cycle frequency and conjugate the result.  For real-valued $x$, the two branches coincide at the same cycle frequency.  We use the unstarred branch from here on and write
\begin{equation}
M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\left\langle x(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\label{eq:cyclicmeanct}
\end{equation}

For a sampled finite record, estimate this coefficient with the $N$-sample average below.  Treating that finite-record average as an estimate of the ensemble cyclic mean also assumes a shared phase reference and convergence of the time average to the ensemble coefficient.

\section*{The Core Operation: Counter-Rotate, Then Average}

Consider the complex record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, and let $\alpha$ be the candidate frequency in cycles per sample, understood modulo one.  An ordinary mean extracts zero frequency, so a nonzero-frequency component tends to cancel as its phase rotates.  Counter-rotation moves a candidate component at $\alpha$ to zero frequency:
\begin{equation*}
z_{\alpha}[n]\equiv x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\end{equation*}
For the unweighted rectangular observation used here, the cyclic-mean estimate is the ordinary mean of the corrected samples:
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
\equiv\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z_{\alpha}[n]
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\label{eq:cyclicmean}
\end{equation}
Here, $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ is the finite-record estimate, and the decision statistic $T_{\alpha}$ is defined below.  For $x[n]=ae^{j2\pi\alpha n}$, every corrected sample equals $a$, so $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=a$.  At a mismatched frequency, the corrected samples still rotate and partially cancel.  This estimator is also used directly in cyclostationary signal detection and classification~\cite[eq. (9)]{dobre2009}.

\textbf{Fourier and correlator views.}
Writing the phase-correction weights as vectors makes the connection explicit.  Collect the record and candidate phase progression in
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vect{x}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
x[0] & x[1] & \cdots & x[N-1]
\end{bmatrix}^{T},\\
\vect{s}_{\alpha}
&=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & e^{j2\pi\alpha} & \cdots & e^{j2\pi\alpha(N-1)}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The entries of $\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}$ are the phase-correction factors in~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean}.  Let $X_N(\alpha)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}$ denote the length-$N$ DTFT evaluation.  The three views are therefore
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\frac{X_N(\alpha)}{N}
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}{N}.
\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
On the DFT grid, $\alpha\equiv k/N\pmod 1$, so $X_N(\alpha)=X[k]$ and $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=X[k]/N$, where
\begin{equation}
X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}.
\end{equation}
Off grid, $X_N(\alpha)$ is still an exact DTFT sample rather than an $N$-point DFT bin.  Zero padding supplies exact DTFT samples on a denser grid, while interpolation or refinement estimates an arbitrary off-grid location.  This is the usual FFT-grid issue, not a limitation of the first-order identity.  Gardner's time-average Fourier coefficients give the corresponding infinite-record view~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.

\section*{When the Common Output Is a GLRT}

None of the identities above requires a noise model.  The GLRT interpretation requires the following signal and noise model~\cite[Sec. 13.4.1]{kay1998}:
\begin{align}
\mathcal H_0 &: \vect{x}=\vect{w},\nonumber\\
\mathcal H_1 &: \vect{x}=a\vect{s}_{\alpha}+\vect{w},
\qquad
\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\sigma^2\vect{I}),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
Here, $\alpha$ and $\sigma^2$ are known, while the deterministic complex amplitude $a$ is unknown.  The noise is proper complex white Gaussian.  Apart from constants, the log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\ell(a)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
\left\|\vect{x}-a\vect{s}_{\alpha}\right\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Because $\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}=N$, maximizing the likelihood gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat a
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\widehat M_x^{\alpha},
\label{eq:ahat}
\end{equation}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:ahat} gives a GLRT that is monotone in
\begin{equation}
T_{\alpha}
=\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}
{\sigma^2\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\frac{N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}.
\label{eq:glrt}
\end{equation}

This result is exact for any finite $N$.  Under~\eqref{eq:model}, the same number is noise-normalized cyclic-mean energy and the usual noncoherent GLRT statistic for a tone.  This tone-detection connection is also developed from matched-filter and Fourier viewpoints in~\cite[p. 600]{spooner2009}.

Under $\mathcal H_0$, the conventional covariance-normalized test formed from the real and imaginary parts of $\widehat M_x^{\alpha}$ is $2T_{\alpha}$~\cite[eqs. (B.1)--(B.3)]{dobre2009}.  The factor of two only rescales the threshold, so the rejection region is unchanged.

Under $\mathcal H_0$, $T_{\alpha}$ has a unit-mean exponential distribution, so
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{FA}}=e^{-\gamma},
\qquad
\gamma=-\log P_{\mathrm{FA}}.
\label{eq:pfa}
\end{equation}
\begin{samepage}
The direct implementation has three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Counter-rotate and sum: $u_{\alpha}=\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}$.
\item Normalize: $T_{\alpha}=|u_{\alpha}|^2/(N\sigma^2)$.
\item Threshold: decide $\mathcal H_1$ when $T_{\alpha}>\gamma$.
\end{enumerate}
This threshold is exact for one fixed $\alpha$ and known $\sigma^2$.  Estimating $\sigma^2$ changes the null distribution and threshold.
\end{samepage}
Under $\mathcal H_1$,
\begin{equation}
2T_{\alpha}\sim
\chi'^2_2\!\left(\frac{2N|a|^2}{\sigma^2}\right),
\label{eq:noncentral}
\end{equation}
which gives the standard noncoherent matched-filter detection probability.  Classical tone-detection performance expressions therefore apply directly to $T_{\alpha}$.

\section*{Numerical Verification and Grid Mismatch}

For a length-$64$ complex tone in white noise, computing $T_{\alpha}$ from the cyclic mean or the matched filter gives the same result to numerical precision.  Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(a) compares the simulated detection probability with the predicted noncentral chi-square law at $P_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.01$, using per-sample SNR $\rho=|a|^2/\sigma^2$.  The repository script reproduces both panels and is available at \url{https://github.com/djgormley/fos}.

Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(b) shows the familiar FFT-grid mismatch.  For the rectangular $N=64$ record, a tone halfway between two bins retains the exact power fraction $[N\sin(\pi/(2N))]^{-2}$, a $3.92$-dB loss when evaluated at one bin.  Evaluating the cyclic mean at the tone's true frequency has no mismatch loss.  Zero padding, interpolation, or frequency refinement provides the same remedies used in higher-order cyclic-spectrum processing.

\section*{Where the Equivalence Needs Care}

The finite-record identity remains exact; the following points affect how its output is used or interpreted.

\textbf{Unknown frequency.}
If the frequency is unknown, maximize~\eqref{eq:glrt} over a candidate set.  This gives a bank of tone matched filters, or a peak-periodogram detector; an FFT performs the search on the DFT grid.  Because several frequencies are tested, the single-frequency threshold in~\eqref{eq:pfa} no longer applies.

\textbf{Colored noise.}
For proper complex $\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\vect{C})$ with known positive-definite covariance $\vect{C}$, whitening the record and tone template gives
\begin{equation}
T_C=
\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{x}|^2}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}.
\label{eq:colored}
\end{equation}
The unweighted cyclic-mean or DFT output remains algebraically equivalent to the tone correlation but is generally not the optimal detector.

\textbf{Random ensemble phase.}
A finite record can contain a detectable tone even when its ensemble cyclic mean is zero.  Let
\begin{equation}
X[n]=Ae^{j(2\pi\alpha_0 n+\Phi)}+W[n],
\qquad \Phi\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi),
\label{eq:randomphase}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is deterministic, $W[n]$ is zero mean, and $\Phi$ is drawn independently for each realization but held fixed within it.  Because $\E\{e^{j\Phi}\}=0$, we have $\E\{X[n]\}=0$.  Within one realization, however, the phase is fixed and, ignoring noise,
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}=Ae^{j\Phi},
\end{equation}
and $|\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}|^2$ remains detectable.  Thus, complex averaging across independently phased records tends to zero, whereas averaging squared magnitudes preserves the tone energy.

\section*{Why the Connection Is Useful}

Second- and higher-order cyclic statistics can reveal periodic dependence when the ordinary mean is zero~\cite[eqs. (8.1)--(8.3)]{gardner2006}.  First order instead describes spectral-line components in the mean~\cite[eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}; such features have been used to detect and classify AM and FSK signals~\cite{dobre2009}.

For a carrier, pilot, residual line, or other first-order component, the identity permits an FFT or tone correlator and brings standard mismatch analysis, thresholds, and performance expressions with it.  It also provides an exact implementation benchmark.  A missed feature then points to familiar causes---frequency mismatch, colored noise, or phase incoherence; independent phases may call for magnitude-square combining or a higher-order cyclic statistic.

\section*{Conclusion}

At first order, the reduced-dimension cyclic moment is the cyclic mean.  For a rectangular $N$-sample record, its finite-record estimate is
\begin{equation*}
\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)
\equiv\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\frac{X_N(\alpha)}{N}
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}{N}.
\end{equation*}
In words, estimating the first-order cyclic moment, evaluating the finite-record DTFT, and correlating with a complex tone produce the same complex output after normalization.

\begin{samepage}
Under the model in~\eqref{eq:model}, the normalized energy of this common output is the matched-filter GLRT statistic:
\begin{equation*}
\boxed{
T_{\alpha}
=\frac{N|\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)|^2}{\sigma^2}.
}
\end{equation*}
\end{samepage}
Thus, the tone matched-filter GLRT statistic is the first-order cyclic-moment energy measured in noise units.  This equality holds at every evaluated $\alpha$; only the $N$-point DFT-bin shortcut requires $\alpha\equiv k/N\pmod 1$.  Equivalently, $|\widehat R_{x,1}^{\alpha}(0)|=(\sigma/\sqrt{N})\sqrt{T_{\alpha}}$, so $T_{\alpha}$ determines the estimate's magnitude but not its phase.

The practical lesson is simple: under the stated model, first-order cyclic detection is ordinary tone detection written in cyclic-statistics notation.
