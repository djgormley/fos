\section*{}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{yclostationary} signal processing and classical detection theory use different language for a particularly simple operation.  The first literature calls it estimation of a first-order cyclic moment.  The second calls it correlation with a complex sinusoid, a Fourier coefficient, or the output of a tone matched filter.  For a finite data record these are not merely analogous operations: they are the same inner product.  Under a known-frequency deterministic-tone model in white Gaussian noise, the usual covariance-normalized first-order cyclic-mean test is also, up to a positive scale factor, the quadrature matched-filter generalized likelihood-ratio test (GLRT).  The algebraic identity is elementary, but its limits matter.  A DFT coefficient is exact only on the DFT grid; colored noise requires whitening; a real sinusoid occupies a two-dimensional quadrature subspace; and an ensemble-random phase can erase the first-order cyclic mean without erasing finite-record tone energy.  This article makes the detector-level equivalence explicit, connects results that are normally presented in separate literatures, and gives practical rules for when the three descriptions can and cannot be interchanged.

\section*{Why the identity is easy to overlook}

Classical wide-sense cyclostationarity is formulated through periodic second-order moments and spectral correlation.  For complex time series, conjugate and nonconjugate autocorrelations provide the relevant second-order coefficients; lag-product processing converts their periodic components into spectral lines~\cite[Secs. 3.3--3.5, pp. 646--649; Sec. 8.1, p. 663]{gardner2006}.  Higher-order cyclic moments and cumulants extend the construction~\cite[Sec. 13.2, pp. 667--669]{gardner2006}.

First order is simpler.  For a real time series, Gardner shows that a finite additive sinusoid is equivalent to a nonzero time-average Fourier coefficient and calls this first-order periodicity; he then derives the finite-window exponential extractor~\cite[Sec. 2.1, pp. 15--16, eqs. (1)--(3); Sec. 2.2, p. 18, eqs. (17)--(21)]{gardner1986}.  Dobre, Rajan, and Inkol later used first-order cyclostationarity for joint detection and classification~\cite[Sec. 4.1, pp. 4--5]{dobre2009}.  Their estimator is Eq. (9)~\cite[Sec. 3.1, eq. (9), p. 3]{dobre2009}, and they later observed that it ``practically represents'' the DFT of the data sequence~\cite[Sec. 6.2.1, p. 7]{dobre2009}.  For the complex deterministic-tone model, Kay derives the unknown-amplitude-and-phase GLRT as a periodogram, or quadrature matched filter~\cite[Sec. 13.8.1, pp. 510--514, eqs. (13.49)--(13.53)]{kay1998}.  Spooner and Nicholls likewise call tone detection a variant of matched-filter detection implementable by matched filtering or Fourier analysis~\cite[Sec. 18.1.4, p. 600]{spooner2009}.

In the literature reviewed for this article, these sources supply the individual links, but none states the complete normalization-aware detector identity.  The contribution here is therefore a clarification and synthesis, not a priority claim for the underlying algebra:

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{deepblue}{warmgray}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\textbf{Central claim.} The finite-record first-order cyclic-mean estimate at one cycle frequency is a normalized complex-sinusoid matched-filter output.  In white Gaussian noise, its covariance-normalized magnitude square has exactly the same rejection regions as the unknown-complex-amplitude tone GLRT.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

The wording is deliberately narrow.  Neither every first-order procedure nor every tone detector is covered, and ``optimal'' is meaningful only after the signal and noise model have been stated.

\section*{From an arbitrary cyclic order to order one}

A general $p$th-order cyclic temporal cross-moment can be written in the form~\cite[Sec. 13.2.1, eq. (13.1), p. 667]{gardner2006}
\begin{equation}
R_{x,p}^{\alpha}(\vect{\tau})
=
\left\langle
\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}
x_{\ell}^{(*)_{\ell}}(t+\tau_{\ell})
e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}
\right\rangle_t ,
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
where $(*)_{\ell}$ specifies whether the corresponding factor is conjugated, $\vect{\tau}$ collects the lags, and $\langle\cdot\rangle_t$ denotes a time average.  Set $p=1$, choose the unconjugated factor, and set its lag to zero.  The result is the cyclic mean
\begin{equation}
M_x^{\alpha}
=\left\langle x(t)e^{-j2\pi\alpha t}\right\rangle_t .
\label{eq:cyclicmeanct}
\end{equation}

For the complex discrete-time record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, with $\alpha$ measured in cycles per sample, the natural finite-record estimate is
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_x^{\alpha}
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi\alpha n}.
\label{eq:cyclicmean}
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
\vect{s}_{\alpha}
=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & e^{j2\pi\alpha} & \cdots & e^{j2\pi\alpha(N-1)}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{equation}
Then, for every finite $N$,
\begin{equation}
N\widehat M_x^{\alpha}=\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}.
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:identity} is the entire algebraic bridge.  Its right-hand side is the correlation with a length-$N$ complex sinusoid, hence a matched-filter output apart from scaling and time-origin convention.  It is also a sample of the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT).  If $\alpha=k/N$, it is exactly the DFT coefficient $X[k]$ under the convention
\begin{equation}
X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}.
\end{equation}
For arbitrary $\alpha$, calling it a DFT bin is imprecise: it is an off-grid DTFT correlation unless a denser filter bank or interpolation is used.

\section*{The detector is the same detector}

The Fourier identity alone does not establish optimality.  For that, consider the one-column complex linear model~\cite[Sec. 13.4.1, pp. 484--485, eqs. (13.19)--(13.20)]{kay1998}:
\begin{align}
\mathcal H_0 &: \vect{x}=\vect{w},\nonumber\\
\mathcal H_1 &: \vect{x}=a\vect{s}_{\alpha}+\vect{w},
\qquad
\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\sigma^2\vect{I}),
\label{eq:model}
\end{align}
where the normalized frequency $\alpha$ is known and the deterministic complex amplitude $a$ is unknown.  The log likelihood, apart from constants, is
\begin{equation}
\ell(a)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
\left\|\vect{x}-a\vect{s}_{\alpha}\right\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Completing the square and using $\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}=N$ gives the maximum-likelihood estimate
\begin{equation}
\widehat a
=\frac{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\widehat M_x^{\alpha},
\label{eq:ahat}
\end{equation}
Substitution of~\eqref{eq:ahat} into the likelihood yields a GLRT monotone in
\begin{equation}
T_{\alpha}
=\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}
{\sigma^2\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}
=\frac{N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}.
\label{eq:glrt}
\end{equation}

This proves an exact finite-sample statement: the magnitude-squared, single-cycle-frequency first-order cyclic-mean detector, the periodogram tone detector, and the quadrature matched-filter GLRT define identical rejection regions under~\eqref{eq:model}.  No asymptotic approximation is involved.

The same conclusion follows from the covariance-normalized form used in statistical cyclic-feature tests~\cite[Appendix B, eqs. (B.1)--(B.3), p. 11]{dobre2009}.  Let
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\vect{m}}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{Re}\{\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\} &
\operatorname{Im}\{\widehat M_x^{\alpha}\}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{equation}
Under $\mathcal H_0$,
$\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{\vect{m}})=\sigma^2\vect{I}_2/(2N)$, so
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\vect{m}}^{T}
\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{\vect{m}})^{-1}
\widehat{\vect{m}}
=\frac{2N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2}{\sigma^2}
=2T_{\alpha}.
\end{equation}
The positive factor of two merely rescales the threshold.  For one channel, Kay's complex-envelope sinusoid GLRT uses this same statistic with the corresponding factor-of-two threshold convention~\cite[Sec. 13.8.1, pp. 510--514, eqs. (13.49)--(13.53)]{kay1998}.

The null distribution provides an immediate threshold rule.  With the complex-noise convention in~\eqref{eq:model}, $T_{\alpha}$ is unit-mean exponential under $\mathcal H_0$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{FA}}=e^{-\gamma},
\qquad
\gamma=-\log P_{\mathrm{FA}}.
\label{eq:pfa}
\end{equation}
Under $\mathcal H_1$,
\begin{equation}
2T_{\alpha}\sim
\chi'^2_2\!\left(\frac{2N|a|^2}{\sigma^2}\right),
\end{equation}
which gives the usual noncoherent matched-filter detection probability.  Thus classical tone-detection performance formulas transfer directly to this first-order cyclic test.

If $a$ including its phase is known, the Neyman-Pearson statistic is instead the coherent quantity
$\operatorname{Re}\{a^*\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}\}$.  The cyclic mean remains the sufficient complex matched-filter output; the appropriate scalar decision statistic changes with the nuisance-parameter model.

\section*{A numerical identity and a practical grid penalty}

Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(a) verifies the common statistic for a length-$64$ complex tone in white noise.  The simulation computes the first-order cyclic estimate and the matched-filter statistic separately; their maximum absolute numerical difference over the direct identity check is below $5\times10^{-12}$.  The plotted Monte Carlo decisions therefore coincide, and the result follows the noncentral chi-square law derived above.

Figure~\ref{fig:verification}(b) illustrates a qualification hidden by the phrase ``the DFT is the detector.''  A single DFT bin is matched only to its grid frequency.  With a rectangular length-$N$ record, an offset of half a bin reduces the nearest-bin output by approximately $3.92$ dB.  The first-order cyclic estimate evaluated at the actual $\alpha$ remains the exactly tuned tone correlation.  An FFT implementation must consequently use a sufficiently dense grid, interpolation, or a subsequent frequency-refinement step when $\alpha$ is not known to lie on the DFT grid.

\section*{Where the equivalence needs qualification}

\textbf{Unknown frequency.}
If frequency is unknown, maximizing~\eqref{eq:glrt} over a candidate set gives
\begin{equation}
T=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal A}
\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{x}|^2}{N\sigma^2}.
\end{equation}
This is a bank of tone matched filters or a peak-periodogram detector.  It is an FFT-bin search only when $\mathcal A$ is the DFT grid.  The false-alarm threshold must also account for the frequency search rather than applying~\eqref{eq:pfa} as if only one independent frequency had been tested.

\textbf{Colored noise.}
For $\vect{w}\sim\CN(\vect{0},\vect{C})$, the GLRT becomes
\begin{equation}
T_C=
\frac{|\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{x}|^2}
{\vect{s}_{\alpha}^{H}\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{s}_{\alpha}}.
\label{eq:colored}
\end{equation}
This is the matched filter after whitening.  The raw cyclic mean or raw DFT coefficient is generally not optimal.  It remains optimal when $\vect{C}^{-1}\vect{s}_{\alpha}$ is proportional to $\vect{s}_{\alpha}$, which includes white noise and some idealized circulant covariance models.

\textbf{Real tones.}
A real sinusoid has positive- and negative-frequency components.  Detection with unknown amplitude and phase projects onto the sine-cosine subspace.  At a non-DC, non-Nyquist DFT bin those two quadratures are orthogonal, and the statistic reduces to a scaled $|X[k]|^2$.  Off grid, the Gram-matrix normalization of the two regressors should be retained rather than assumed diagonal.

\textbf{Multiple harmonics.}
A general periodic mean can contain several Fourier components.  Testing its complete first-order signature is then a matched-subspace problem, not a single matched filter.  If the harmonics lie on distinct DFT bins in white noise, the joint statistic is the sum of their normalized bin energies.  The single-$\alpha$ identity applies to each component separately.

\textbf{Windowing.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cyclicmean} uses a rectangular observation interval.  A tapered cyclic estimate is still a windowed sinusoidal correlator, but it is no longer the white-noise matched filter for a constant-envelope, rectangularly observed tone unless that taper models the actual signal envelope.  Tapering exchanges coherent SNR for reduced leakage.

\textbf{Random ensemble phase.}
The most important conceptual boundary concerns what is meant by ``first-order cyclostationarity.''  Consider
\begin{equation}
X[n]=Ae^{j(2\pi\alpha_0 n+\Phi)}+W[n],
\qquad \Phi\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi),
\label{eq:randomphase}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is drawn once and held fixed within each realization.  Ensemble averaging gives $\E\{X[n]\}=0$, so the ensemble first-order cyclic mean vanishes.  Conditional on the realized phase and ignoring noise, however,
\begin{equation}
\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}=Ae^{j\Phi},
\end{equation}
and $|\widehat M_X^{\alpha_0}|^2$ remains fully detectable.  Complex averaging across independently phase-randomized records can cancel; noncoherent magnitude-square combining does not.

Accordingly, the assumption-free identity is between the \emph{finite-record cyclic-mean estimate} and the tone correlator.  Equating tone presence with a nonzero \emph{ensemble} first-order cyclic mean additionally requires a phase reference or an appropriate first-order cycloergodicity condition.

\section*{Why second and higher orders receive more attention}

The relative prominence of second-order and higher-order cyclostationarity does not imply that first order was unknown.  First order identifies a periodic mean or spectral line~\cite[Sec. 2.1, pp. 15--16, eqs. (1)--(3)]{gardner1986}.  Second-order processing creates spectral lines from lag products of the signal, exposing periodic covariance structure even when the ordinary mean is zero~\cite[Sec. 8.1, p. 663, eqs. (8.1)--(8.3)]{gardner2006}.  Estimation and testing methods, including higher-order extensions, are developed in broader cyclic-statistics frameworks~\cite[Secs. 4.1, 11, and 13.2]{gardner2006}.

The first-order case therefore sits at an unusually simple intersection.  Cyclostationary terminology emphasizes the Fourier coefficient of the time-varying mean; Fourier terminology emphasizes a spectral line; detection terminology emphasizes correlation with a known waveform.  None of these views is more fundamental.  Under the stated model they are coordinate systems for the same sufficient statistic.

\section*{Practical takeaways}

\begin{itemize}
\item A single-cycle-frequency first-order cyclic estimator does not require a specialized implementation.  It can be computed as a complex mixer followed by an accumulator, as a DTFT evaluation, or as an FFT bin when the frequency is on grid.
\item Matched-filter threshold and performance results can be reused directly after aligning normalizations.  For the complex white-noise model used here, $N|\widehat M_x^{\alpha}|^2/\sigma^2$ is unit-mean exponential under the null.
\item ``DFT,'' ``matched filter,'' and ``first-order cyclic detector'' should not be counted as three independent detectors in a comparison when they use the same record, frequency, window, and normalization.  They are the same statistic with different labels.
\item The implementation should change when the assumptions change: search or interpolate for unknown frequency, whiten for colored noise, use two real quadratures for a real tone, and use a subspace statistic for multiple harmonics.
\item A nonzero finite-record tone statistic need not imply a nonzero ensemble cyclic mean.  Phase coherence and averaging conventions must be stated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Concluding perspective}

The algebraic reduction from first-order cyclostationarity to a Fourier coefficient is immediate.  Its detector interpretation is equally exact once the statistical model is made explicit.  For a known-frequency complex tone of unknown amplitude in white Gaussian noise, the standard covariance-normalized first-order cyclic-mean test is, up to scale, the periodogram and quadrature matched-filter GLRT.  Gardner's first-order periodicity result, Dobre et al.'s DFT-implemented first-order cyclic estimator and covariance-normalized test, and classical complex-sinusoid detection theory contain the constituent pieces.  Writing the pieces as one proposition is useful because it removes redundant terminology, imports mature matched-filter performance theory into the first-order cyclic setting, and exposes the assumptions that are otherwise easy to overlook.  The identity is simple; knowing precisely when to use it is the practical trick.
